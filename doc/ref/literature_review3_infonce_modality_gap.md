# InfoNCE, Modality Gaps, and Cross‑Embodiment Transfer in Locomotion and Robotics

The statement that “InfoNCE has a specific history of failing to transfer across bodies, even when intended to be shared” is not literally present as a settled claim in the literature, but it does capture, in informal language, several documented geometric and optimization pathologies of InfoNCE that are directly relevant to cross‑embodiment and morphology‑agnostic representation learning. Work on multimodal representation learning shows that standard InfoNCE with independent encoders systematically produces **modality gaps**, in which different data sources occupy separated regions of the shared latent space despite strong pairwise alignment, and this effect has been mathematically analyzed and empirically confirmed in CLIP‑style systems.[Closing the Modality Gap Aligns Group-W...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18525v1>)[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>)[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>)[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>) At the same time, several robotics papers demonstrate that **carefully designed InfoNCE‑based objectives can successfully align latent action spaces or motion representations across distinct robot embodiments**, enabling multi‑robot control and cross‑embodiment skill transfer in manipulation and motion retargeting tasks.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Iii Experiments](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15419v1>)[End-to-end Learning for Multi-](<https://www.hkhlr.de/en/node-export/pdf/3631>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>) The most accurate reading of the current evidence is therefore nuanced: vanilla contrastive InfoNCE with naive negative sampling and independent encoders tends to create distribution‑level separations that can hinder morphology‑agnostic sharing, but InfoNCE itself is not intrinsically unable to transfer across bodies; its failure or success depends critically on encoder architecture, negative sampling geometry, auxiliary losses (such as reconstruction), and task design.

## Cross‑Embodiment Locomotion and the Need for Shared Latent Representations

### Cross‑Embodiment as a Morphology and Modality Problem

Your project focuses on cross‑embodiment locomotion: learning a morphology‑agnostic latent action and world‑model representation from egocentric video so that the locomotion behaviour of one robot (for example, an 18‑DOF stick insect) can drive another robot (such as a 12‑DOF Unitree B1 quadruped) via a shared body‑motion coordinate system, specifically Froude‑scaled forward, lateral, and yaw components. This is done with minimal prior structural information, explicitly avoiding kinematic models, URDF descriptions, and explicit demonstrations on the target robot, and instead relying on visual prediction and latent dynamics learned from egocentric views.

Conceptually, this cross‑embodiment setting is close to a multimodal learning problem. Each morphology induces a different distribution of visual observations and actions, even if they are grounded in the same underlying physical variables like center‑of‑mass motion or ground reaction forces. In a naïve multi‑encoder InfoNCE setup, the stick insect’s egocentric video frames and the B1’s egocentric video frames would be treated as two “modalities” that are projected into a shared latent space, and paired sequences (for example, time‑aligned Froude trajectories or retargeted motion snippets) would be aligned with a contrastive objective. This is structurally analogous to CLIP‑style image‑text learning, where images and texts are encoded independently and aligned via InfoNCE.[Closing the Modality Gap Aligns Group-W...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18525v1>)[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>)

The goal in cross‑embodiment locomotion, however, is more demanding than instance‑wise retrieval. You need a latent representation that is not only able to match single sequences across bodies but that also preserves **group‑wise semantics**: for example, all “trot” gaits across embodiments should occupy a coherent region of latent space, enabling policy transfer, and the manifold geometry should allow smooth interpolation and predictive modelling over long horizons. This requirement makes the known modality‑gap phenomena of InfoNCE acutely relevant.

### Latent Actions, World Models, and Long‑Horizon Stability

In your current pipeline, you already have evidence that Froude‑scaled body‑motion coordinates can be transferred across bodies and that some future‑trajectory models (FTMs) can predict coarse behaviour up to horizons \(k \approx 10\) for both robots, making these FTMs candidates for controller signals. The bottleneck, as you noted, lies in the reinforcement learning optimizer, particularly in actor‑critic stability and long‑horizon value estimation, with phenomena reminiscent of the Koopman Dreamer failure mode where prediction error accumulates across the planning horizon in periodic locomotion tasks.

Although Dreamer‑style world models usually rely on reconstruction + KL losses rather than InfoNCE, the deeper issue is still one of latent space geometry and predictive stability. For cross‑embodiment transfer, the latent world model must simultaneously satisfy three constraints: it must be predictive under saturation‑free dynamics across horizons; it must be morphology‑agnostic enough that the same latent coordinates drive different bodies coherently; and it must be learnable with realistic sample sizes, without collapsing to degenerate encodings. A contrastive InfoNCE loss is one possible mechanism to enforce alignment and uniformity in such a latent space, but as the literature shows, it comes with specific geometric biases that can either help or hinder morphology‑agnostic sharing depending on configuration.[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>)[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>)[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>)

Your question therefore touches precisely the intersection of InfoNCE’s known limitations and its documented successes: is it true, in other people’s papers, that InfoNCE is “known” not to transfer across bodies? To answer this, it is necessary to review both the theoretical behaviour of InfoNCE and the empirical evidence in robotics and locomotion.

## InfoNCE: Objective, Mutual Information Interpretation, and Geometric Behaviour

### Standard InfoNCE Objective and Mutual Information Bounds

The InfoNCE loss is a contrastive learning objective widely used to learn representations by aligning positive pairs of samples and repelling negatives.[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[Contrastive Learning for Retrieval: Inf...](<https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/contrastive-learning-retrieval-infonce-dpr>) Given an anchor representation \(z_{x}\) and a positive representation \(z_{x^{+}}\) (for example, two augmented views of the same image, or an image and its caption), together with a set of negative representations \(z_{x'}\) sampled from other data points, the standard InfoNCE loss is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = - \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_{x}, z_{x^{+}})/\tau)}{\sum_{x'} \exp(\text{sim}(z_{x}, z_{x'})/\tau)}.[H-InfoNCE Loss: Enhancing Contrastive L...](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/h-infonce-loss>)[Contrastive InfoNCE Loss Overview](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/contrastive-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)
\]

Here \(\text{sim}\) is usually cosine similarity and \(\tau > 0\) is a temperature hyperparameter that controls the sharpness of the softmax distribution.[H-InfoNCE Loss: Enhancing Contrastive L...](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/h-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[Contrastive Learning for Retrieval: Inf...](<https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/contrastive-learning-retrieval-infonce-dpr>) The loss encourages \(\text{sim}(z_{x}, z_{x^{+}})\) to become large relative to the similarities with all negatives.

From an information‑theoretic point of view, InfoNCE provides a lower bound on mutual information between the anchor and its positive.[Contrastive InfoNCE Loss Overview](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/contrastive-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[PI-ARS: Accelerating Evolution-Learned...](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13224.pdf>) In the classic contrastive predictive coding formulation, InfoNCE is used as a variational bound on predictive information, such as \(I(\text{past}; \text{future})\) between past and future latent states in a time series or control task.[PI-ARS: Accelerating Evolution-Learned...](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13224.pdf>) In the context of visual locomotion with predictive representations, this interpretation has been used explicitly to learn encoders that maximize predictive information between past and future observations; specifically, a scalar function \(f\) parameterizes density ratios and the InfoNCE‑style form

\[
\tilde{I}(\text{past}; \text{future obs}) = \mathbb{E}_\tau\left[f(\phi(s_t), \phi_v(s_{t+k}^{v})) - \log \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{s}_{t+k}} \exp(f(\phi(s_t), \phi_v(\tilde{s}_{t+k}^{v})))\right]
\]

is maximized with respect to encoder \(\phi\) and critic \(f\), using randomly sampled future states \(\tilde{s}_{t+k}^{v}\) as negatives.[PI-ARS: Accelerating Evolution-Learned...](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13224.pdf>) This shows that InfoNCE is not confined to static multimodal alignment: it has been practically adopted in locomotion to learn predictive world models for visual policies.

However, more recent theoretical work clarifies that InfoNCE does not maximize mutual information directly so much as a particular lower bound that involves both positive and negative contributions. In cross‑modal contrastive learning, it has been shown that optimizing InfoNCE is equivalent to maximizing a lower bound on the difference between two mutual information terms: \( \text{MI-P} - \text{MI-N} \), where MI‑P is the mutual information between anchors and positives, and MI‑N is the mutual information between anchors and negatives.[Vision Language Pre-training by Contras...](<https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.04474>) This means that, in addition to increasing the dependence between anchor and positive representations, InfoNCE tends to drive the MI between anchors and negatives towards zero, treating all negatives as if they should be unrelated, even if some of them are **false negatives** that share semantic content with the anchor.[Vision Language Pre-training by Contras...](<https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.04474>)

### Alignment–Uniformity Perspective and False Negatives

Empirically, InfoNCE can be interpreted as implementing a combination of **alignment** and **uniformity** in latent space: alignment pulls positive pairs together, while uniformity pushes all representations to spread out on the unit hypersphere, avoiding collapse.[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>) This view is useful but incomplete. Analytical studies of InfoNCE in multimodal settings show that the alignment and uniformity components can conflict, particularly when multiple modalities are involved and when label or view noise introduces false negatives.[Contrastive InfoNCE Loss Overview](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/contrastive-infonce-loss>)[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>)[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>)

When the negative sampling ratio increases, the InfoNCE bound on mutual information becomes tighter, which should be beneficial in clean label regimes.[Contrastive InfoNCE Loss Overview](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/contrastive-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>) Yet under realistic conditions with label or view noise, increasing the number of negatives saturates performance and can even degrade it, because more negatives mean more potential false negatives—samples that actually share semantics with the anchor but are treated as negatives.[Contrastive InfoNCE Loss Overview](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/contrastive-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>) Empirical studies show a non‑monotonic relationship between performance and the proportion of negatives: performance peaks at a moderate negative ratio and then declines as false negatives introduce harmful gradient signals, contaminating the learning process.[Contrastive InfoNCE Loss Overview](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/contrastive-infonce-loss>) This sensitivity to false negatives is highly relevant to cross‑embodiment learning, where different bodies may produce visually distinct but semantically equivalent behaviours and where naive in‑batch negative sampling can easily label related samples as negatives.

### Temperature Sensitivity and Mode Failure

The temperature \(\tau\) in InfoNCE modulates the concentration of the softmax distribution over similarities. When \(\tau\) is too small, the softmax becomes extremely sharp, and gradients vanish except near the optimum; when \(\tau\) is too large, the loss cannot produce sharp separation between positives and negatives.[H-InfoNCE Loss: Enhancing Contrastive L...](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/h-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[Contrastive Learning for Retrieval: Inf...](<https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/contrastive-learning-retrieval-infonce-dpr>) Beyond these optimization issues, recent work shows that low temperatures can induce more profound geometric failures.

In dual‑encoder multimodal settings with independent encoders, InfoNCE at low temperature has been shown to **actively generate modality gaps**, where different modalities (e.g., images and texts) occupy distinct submanifolds in latent space despite being trained together.[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>) Analyses of symmetric multimodal InfoNCE further reveal that the intrinsic geometry of the induced latent manifold includes a negative symmetric divergence term that structurally favours modality‑level separation under conditional heterogeneity.[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>) This implies that InfoNCE does not merely fail to fully mix modalities by accident; under certain regimes it has a **mode‑failure**, systematically encouraging population‑level separation while still aligning individual pairs via high cosine similarity.[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>)[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>)

This behaviour is directly relevant to the idea that InfoNCE “does not transfer across bodies.” If each robot embodiment is treated as a separate modality, then a modality gap means that their latent distributions will remain separated at a population level, even though instance‑wise alignment between paired samples (for example, retargeted motions) might succeed. For morphology‑agnostic locomotion, this population‑level separation can be fatal: it obstructs shared policy learning and long‑horizon world‑model transfer.

## InfoNCE in Robotics and Locomotion: Evidence from Existing Work

### Predictive Information Representations for Visual Locomotion (PI‑ARS)

In one line of work on legged robots, InfoNCE is used as a contrastive variational bound to maximize predictive information between past and future observations.[PI-ARS: Accelerating Evolution-Learned...](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13224.pdf>) The encoder \(\phi\) learns representations of high‑dimensional image observations in a legged locomotion setting, and the auxiliary function \(f\) is trained via InfoNCE to estimate mutual information \(I(\text{past}; \text{future obs})\).[PI-ARS: Accelerating Evolution-Learned...](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13224.pdf>) Using these predictive representations, the authors train an optimal policy with augmented random search (ARS), an evolutionary strategy method, and demonstrate that predictive information helps stabilize visual locomotion learning.

Critically, this work does not address cross‑embodiment transfer. It operates on a single morphology and uses InfoNCE to learn a world model that is predictive for that robot’s dynamics. It shows that InfoNCE‑style predictive coding can work in locomotion and can yield useful state representations for control, but it says nothing about whether the learned latent can be shared across different bodies.

The relevance for your question is that InfoNCE, in this context, is clearly capable of capturing dynamics and aiding control, but is only evaluated within one embodiment. Any claim that “InfoNCE fails to transfer across bodies” must therefore be understood as conditional: InfoNCE can succeed in representing dynamics when task and architecture are well matched, but its geometry may not automatically support morphology‑agnostic sharing.

### Contractive Mapping Embeddings for Robust Humanoid Locomotion (CMR)

Another recent locomotion‑related work uses InfoNCE within a composite objective for contractive mapping embeddings in humanoid locomotion on unstructured terrains.[CMR: Contractive Mapping Embeddings for...](<https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2602.03511>) In this framework, semantic discrimination among states is attained via contrastive learning, and the InfoNCE loss is integrated into a total objective that also includes a Lipschitz regularization term and a PPO reinforcement learning loss:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CMR}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{Lipschitz}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}.[CMR: Contractive Mapping Embeddings for...](<https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2602.03511>)
\]

The InfoNCE component is used to enforce discriminative latent states, helping the agent distinguish semantically different terrain and body configurations while the Lipschitz term controls sensitivity and the PPO term drives control performance.[CMR: Contractive Mapping Embeddings for...](<https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2602.03511>) This again demonstrates that InfoNCE can be embedded in locomotion pipelines and contribute to robust behaviour. However, like the PI‑ARS work, this study is conducted for a single humanoid embodiment and does not investigate cross‑embodiment transfer.

From the perspective of your statement, CMR is a counterexample to any blanket claim that “InfoNCE fails in locomotion”: it works in a humanoid locomotion system and contributes to robustness. But it neither supports nor refutes claims about cross‑body transfer; it simply does not address that axis.

### Morphology‑Agnostic Locomotion in Unified Robot Morphology Architectures (URMA)

Morphology‑agnostic locomotion has been studied in frameworks such as URMA (Unified Robot Morphology Architecture).[End-to-end Learning for Multi-](<https://www.hkhlr.de/en/node-export/pdf/3631>) URMA uses a combination of deep reinforcement learning and morphology‑aware neural network architectures to train locomotion policies for multiple robot morphologies simultaneously in simulation, including quadrupeds, hexapods, and humanoids.[End-to-end Learning for Multi-](<https://www.hkhlr.de/en/node-export/pdf/3631>) Special encoders and decoders translate each robot’s specific joint and sensor information into a shared representation space, allowing the policy network to generalize across bodies and control a wide range of robots.[End-to-end Learning for Multi-](<https://www.hkhlr.de/en/node-export/pdf/3631>)

Although the summary does not explicitly state that URMA uses InfoNCE, the architecture relies on shared latent representations and morphology‑aware encoders and decoders to achieve cross‑embodiment generalization.[End-to-end Learning for Multi-](<https://www.hkhlr.de/en/node-export/pdf/3631>) The success of URMA shows that **shared latent action spaces across diverse embodiments are feasible**, but it does not attribute its success to InfoNCE in particular. Instead, it relies on architecture design and joint randomization in simulation.

For your question, URMA is important as a proof of concept that cross‑embodiment locomotion is possible with the right latent and architectural design. Whether InfoNCE is the best objective for learning such a latent remains open.

### Latent Action Spaces for Cross‑Embodiment Manipulation with InfoNCE

The most directly relevant evidence regarding InfoNCE and cross‑embodiment transfer comes from recent work on **latent cross‑embodiment policies**, which unify diverse end‑effector action spaces into a semantically aligned latent space and use InfoNCE‑based contrastive losses to achieve action alignment.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>) In this framework, encoders for different modalities (e.g., anthropomorphic robotic hands, a human hand, and a parallel jaw gripper) are trained to project actions into a shared latent space, using pairwise InfoNCE to ensure alignment within batches of aligned end‑effector poses that have been generated via retargeting.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>)

The contrastive loss in this setting takes the form

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{contrastive}} = \frac{1}{M(M-1)} \sum_{i=1}^{M} \sum_{j=i+1}^{M} \left( -\frac{1}{B} \sum_{n=1}^{B} \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(q_i(x_n^i), q_j(x_n^j))/\tau)}{\sum_{k} \exp(\text{sim}(q_i(x_n^i), q_j(x_k^j))/\tau)} \right),
\]

where \(M\) is the number of modalities (embodiments), \(B\) is the batch size, and \(q_m\) are modality‑specific encoders.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>) This pairwise InfoNCE loss is used to align different modalities’ action embeddings in shared latent space. In the second stage, modality‑specific decoders are trained to reconstruct ground truth actions from latent representations, and the total loss combines reconstruction and contrastive alignment:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{contrastive}}.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>)
\]

Empirically, this framework has been shown to enable **multi‑robot control from a single policy** across substantially different robot morphologies and to yield **up to 25.3% improved manipulation success rates** compared to single‑embodiment policies.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>) In pick‑and‑place experiments with the Faive hand and Franka gripper, cross‑embodiment latent policies achieved around 10% improvement for the Faive hand and 7.5% for the Franka gripper, with reduced variance and robust transfer even across large embodiment gaps.[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>) These results provide **strong positive evidence** that InfoNCE‑based contrastive learning can successfully unify action spaces across embodiments when the encoders and decoders are designed appropriately and when reconstruction loss is used to preserve embodiment‑specific structure alongside alignment.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>)

From the standpoint of your statement, this cross‑embodiment manipulation work is critical: it shows that InfoNCE does not have an inherent, universal failure to transfer across bodies. Rather, it can succeed in doing exactly that when used in a carefully controlled way.

### Cross‑Embodiment Humanoid Motion Retargeting with Triplet Loss

Another relevant line of work focuses on cross‑embodiment humanoid motion retargeting. Here, contrastive learning is used to discover a shared latent space across humans and diverse humanoid platforms, including single‑arm, dual‑arm, and legged humanoid robots.[Iii Experiments](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15419v1>) The authors propose decoupling the latent space into distinct subspaces for different body segments, such as left arm, right arm, trunk, left leg, and right leg.[Iii Experiments](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15419v1>) Each segment has its own latent space, and contrastive learning is applied independently to each subspace.

Importantly, this work uses **triplet loss**, not InfoNCE. For each subspace, randomly sampled triplets consist of an anchor pose, a positive pose that is semantically similar, and a negative pose that is less similar. The triplet loss is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{contrastive}} = \sum_{\mathcal{S}} \sum_{(z_i^o, z_j^+, z_k^-) \in \mathcal{S}} \max(\|z_i^o - z_j^+\|_2 - \|z_i^o - z_k^-\|_2 + \alpha, 0),
\]

where \(\alpha\) is a margin hyperparameter.[Iii Experiments](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15419v1>) This objective enforces a minimum separation between positives and negatives in each subspace and is used to align motions semantically across embodiments.[Iii Experiments](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15419v1>)

While this work does not use InfoNCE, it shows that **metric learning with explicit positive‑negative margins can be effective for cross‑embodiment motion**, especially when the latent space is decomposed into body‑part‑specific subspaces. It indirectly suggests that contrastive learning objectives other than InfoNCE may be more suitable for certain cross‑embodiment tasks, particularly when the geometry of the latent space must simultaneously accommodate multiple embodiments.

## Documented Limitations of InfoNCE Relevant to Cross‑Embodiment

### False Negatives, Label Noise, and Class Imbalance

Across domains, one of the most fundamental limitations of InfoNCE is its sensitivity to **false negatives**, label noise, and class imbalance. In time‑series contrastive learning, it has been shown that InfoNCE‑based strategies often overlook false negatives and class imbalance inherent in instance discrimination tasks.[Time-Series Contrastive Learning agains...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11939>) These issues motivate modifications that construct instance graphs to emulate supervised contrastive learning with multiple‑instance discrimination, thereby mitigating the harmful impact of false negatives and improving representation of minority classes.[Time-Series Contrastive Learning agains...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11939>)

In code search, InfoNCE is widely used to discriminate between relevant and irrelevant code samples given a query, but large code corpora contain numerous duplications, leading to many negative pairs that are in fact false negatives.[Rethinking Negative Pairs in Code Search](<https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2310.08069>) Moreover, standard InfoNCE fails to explicitly differentiate between degrees of relevance among negatives; a bubble sorting algorithm is less negative than a file‑saving function relative to a quicksort query, but InfoNCE treats all negatives equally.[Rethinking Negative Pairs in Code Search](<https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2310.08069>) These problems deteriorate representation learning, and methods such as Soft‑InfoNCE have been proposed to weight negatives according to relevance, setting weights for suspected false negatives to zero and modelling confusability in a continuous fashion.[Rethinking Negative Pairs in Code Search](<https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2310.08069>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)

In recommendation and collaborative filtering, InfoNCE‑based contrastive learning must contend with out‑of‑distribution behaviour, the risk of false negatives among unobserved user‑item interactions, and top‑\(K\) evaluation dynamics.[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>) Standard losses treat all unobserved items as negatives and ignore ranking relationships among negatives, easily overlooking false negatives.[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>) Adversarial variants such as AdvInfoNCE focus more on mining hard negatives and distinguishing false negatives from vast unlabeled interactions.[H-InfoNCE Loss: Enhancing Contrastive L...](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/h-infonce-loss>)[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>) Empirical results demonstrate that AdvInfoNCE can identify a large fraction of false negatives (about 70% in one Tencent dataset) and improve out‑of‑distribution performance.[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>)

These patterns are highly relevant to cross‑embodiment locomotion. When multiple embodiments are trained together using in‑batch negatives, the latent representations for one body’s behaviours may be treated as negatives for another body’s behaviours, even when they are semantically similar. Without careful sampling or weighting, these cross‑embodiment false negatives can push the latent distributions of different bodies apart, reinforcing morphological separation instead of encouraging shared structure.

### Modality Gap and Population Geometry

The **modality gap** literature provides more direct evidence of how InfoNCE can structurally separate populations in latent space even when pairwise alignment succeeds. In CLIP‑style dual‑encoder models, images and texts are encoded separately and aligned with symmetric InfoNCE losses, yet the resulting latent spaces often show pronounced modality gaps: image and text embeddings occupy distinct regions or submanifolds of the joint space rather than forming a fully mixed distribution.[Closing the Modality Gap Aligns Group-W...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18525v1>)[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>)

Analytical work on the geometric mechanics of contrastive learning shows that in unimodal InfoNCE, the intrinsic landscape is strictly convex and Gibbs‑like, favouring uniformity, while in symmetric multimodal InfoNCE, a negative symmetric divergence term emerges.[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>) This divergence term cross‑couples the modalities: each modality’s marginal reshapes the effective landscape of the other, allowing strong pairwise alignment to coexist with a persistent modality gap under conditional heterogeneity.[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>) Controlled experiments on synthetic data and analyses of CLIP representations confirm these predictions: modality gaps appear even when retrieval performance is high, and mild caption corruption enlarges the gap.[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>)

A separate line of work provides theoretical and empirical evidence that InfoNCE with independent encoders has a mode‑failure at low temperatures, generating modality gaps systematically.[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>) Uni‑modal experiments with two independent encoders and identical initialization show that InfoNCE actively generates a gap when \(\tau\) is small, and multimodal experiments confirm large modality distances and near‑perfect linear separability between modalities, even when instance‑level semanticity is limited.[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>) To mitigate the gap, these authors propose xNCE, which samples negative pairs interchangeably from both modalities, mixing intra‑ and inter‑modality negatives to remove the divergence objective that separates modalities.[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>) xNCE reduces the modality gap across temperatures and improves instance‑level semanticity without sacrificing retrieval performance, demonstrating that mixed negatives act as an implicit regularizer promoting cross‑modal overlap.[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>)

Additional work shows that the modality gap is especially problematic for group‑wise semantics, such as clustering.[Closing the Modality Gap Aligns Group-W...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18525v1>) While the gap may be irrelevant for instance‑wise tasks like retrieval that depend on relative rankings, it harms group‑level tasks by increasing within‑group scatter and disrupting absolute distances among multimodal representations in the latent space.[Closing the Modality Gap Aligns Group-W...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18525v1>) A novel objective that explicitly encourages alignment of matching pairs while maintaining uniform latent structure can reduce the gap and improve clustering performance while preserving retrieval accuracy.[Closing the Modality Gap Aligns Group-W...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18525v1>)

From the perspective of cross‑embodiment locomotion, different robot bodies can be treated as separate modalities or subpopulations of a multimodal dataset. The modality‑gap results, therefore, strongly suggest that standard InfoNCE with independent encoders will tend to separate bodies in latent space, hindering morphology‑agnostic sharing. This is not a statement that InfoNCE “cannot transfer” across bodies in principle, but rather that its default geometric tendencies are misaligned with the goal of a fully shared latent.

### Alignment–Uniformity Conflicts in Multimodal Regimes

In multimodal regimes with more than two modalities, InfoNCE’s limitations become even more pronounced. Studies of multimodal representation learning with \(M \geq 3\) modalities identify two specific conflicts exacerbated by InfoNCE: an alignment–uniformity conflict and an intra‑alignment conflict.[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>) The alignment–uniformity conflict arises when the uniformity term, designed to spread embeddings uniformly on the hypersphere, exerts a repulsive force that opposes the attractive force pulling positive multimodal pairs together.[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>) This directional opposition leads to smaller effective gradient updates and exacerbates distributional gaps across modalities.[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>) The intra‑alignment conflict occurs when aligning a single sample’s representation to multiple other modalities simultaneously; if the positive embeddings from different modalities are not perfectly collinear, the alignment directions compete and reduce net attractive force, weakening alignment and widening modality gaps.[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>)

Although these analyses are framed in terms of modalities such as image, text, and audio, the same reasoning applies to multiple robot embodiments, especially when actions or observations are treated as separate channels. Morphology‑agnostic locomotion requires coherent alignment of many embodiments to a common latent, and InfoNCE may face similar alignment‑uniformity and intra‑alignment conflicts, making this task particularly challenging without modifications.

### Hard Negative Mining and Negative Quality

Another dimension relevant to cross‑embodiment is the quality of negatives. Work on contrastive learning for retrieval shows that too‑easy negatives waste training signal, while too‑hard negatives—especially false negatives—can actively hurt the model.[Contrastive Learning for Retrieval: Inf...](<https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/contrastive-learning-retrieval-infonce-dpr>) The key principle is that the model can only learn distinctions it is forced to make, and the quality of negatives determines the richness of the distinctions the training process demands.[Contrastive Learning for Retrieval: Inf...](<https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/contrastive-learning-retrieval-infonce-dpr>) Standard InfoNCE uses in‑batch negatives, reusing positives from other queries as negatives, which makes batch size a crucial hyperparameter.[Contrastive Learning for Retrieval: Inf...](<https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/contrastive-learning-retrieval-infonce-dpr>)

In cross‑embodiment settings, naive in‑batch negative sampling across bodies means that behaviours from one body may serve as negatives for semantically similar behaviours from another body. Unless hard negative mining is carefully controlled and false negatives are mitigated, InfoNCE may penalize precisely the morphological invariances you want to preserve. The literature addresses these issues with H‑InfoNCE variants that emphasize hard negatives and robust optimization, and with Soft‑InfoNCE and AdvInfoNCE approaches that weight or adversarially select negatives.[H-InfoNCE Loss: Enhancing Contrastive L...](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/h-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>) These developments suggest that negative sampling and weighting are critical degrees of freedom for making InfoNCE compatible with morphology‑agnostic transfer.

## Is InfoNCE Intrinsically Non‑Transferable Across Embodiments?

### Clarifying “Transfer Across Bodies” in Latent Space Terms

To assess whether InfoNCE is “known” not to transfer across bodies, it is important to clarify what “transfer” means. For cross‑embodiment locomotion, transfer has at least three levels:

1. **Instance‑wise transfer**: given a behaviour instance in one body, we can find or generate a corresponding behaviour in another body via latent alignment.

2. **Group‑wise transfer**: classes of behaviours (such as gait types) occupy coherent regions of latent space across bodies, enabling clustering, interpolation, and structured policy transfer.

3. **Dynamics‑level transfer**: world‑model dynamics expressed in latent coordinates are shared or at least smoothly mapped across bodies, allowing a policy trained in latent space to be deployed on novel embodiments with minimal adaptation.

The modality gap literature shows that InfoNCE can achieve instance‑wise alignment (e.g., retrieval) while failing at group‑wise semantics and population‑level mixing.[Closing the Modality Gap Aligns Group-W...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18525v1>)[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>)[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>)[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>) Robotics experiments with latent action spaces demonstrate that InfoNCE can achieve instance‑wise alignment and group‑wise transfer across embodiments in manipulation tasks when combined with reconstruction losses and properly designed encoders and decoders.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>) None of these works claim that InfoNCE is incapable of dynamics‑level transfer, but they rarely assess long‑horizon world‑model scaling across embodiments.

Therefore, the statement that “InfoNCE is known not to transfer across bodies” is too strong when taken literally. The precise, evidence‑backed statement would be: **Standard InfoNCE with independent encoders and naive negative sampling has documented geometric tendencies to maintain modality‑ or population‑level gaps, which can hinder morphology‑agnostic latent sharing; however, with suitable architecture and loss design, InfoNCE can be used to build shared latent spaces across distinct robot embodiments.**

### Reading Modality‑Gap Results Through a Morphology Lens

If we re‑interpret modalities as embodiments, the modality‑gap results effectively state that InfoNCE, as commonly used, tends to **separate embodiments in latent space**, even when pairwise alignment between matched samples is strong. In CLIP‑style dual‑encoder systems, images and texts become linearly separable by modality, with large modality distances at low temperatures, and the distribution‑level gap persists even when retrieval metrics are good.[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>)[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>) Analogously, if one naively encodes egocentric video from different robot morphologies with separate encoders and aligns them via InfoNCE using only instance‑wise positives, the resulting latent may exhibit **embodiment gaps**, where each body occupies a distinct submanifold, making shared policies and world models difficult to learn.

This interpretation is supported by geometric analyses that show how symmetric multimodal InfoNCE induces a negative symmetric divergence coupling between modalities, structurally favouring separated marginals under conditional heterogeneity.[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>) In cross‑embodiment locomotion, conditional heterogeneity arises naturally: even when underlying physical quantities like Froude numbers are shared, the mapping between latent and raw observations differs across bodies, and the distribution of egocentric visuals and proprioceptive signals varies.

### Counterexamples: Cross‑Embodiment Successes with InfoNCE

At the same time, cross‑embodiment action‑space alignment work provides **counterexamples** to a universal failure claim. Pairwise InfoNCE across modalities, combined with reconstruction losses and retargeted data, yields a shared latent space in which actions from anthropomorphic hands, human hands, and grippers are semantically aligned, and a single policy can control multiple robots with improved performance over single‑embodiment baselines.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>) These results demonstrate that InfoNCE can provide cross‑embodiment transfer when:

1. Encoders are modality‑specific but trained jointly with pairwise InfoNCE across aligned samples.

2. The latent is constrained by reconstruction losses that preserve embodiment‑specific geometry alongside alignment.

3. Negative sampling is done within retargeted batches where positive matches are known, avoiding uncontrolled cross‑embodiment false negatives.

Thus, InfoNCE can support cross‑embodiment sharing under appropriate conditions.

### Where InfoNCE Likely Fails for Cross‑Embodiment Locomotion

The strongest conclusion one can draw from the literature is that **vanilla InfoNCE with independent encoders and naive in‑batch negatives is ill‑suited to morphology‑agnostic locomotion**, especially when world models must be stable over long horizons and when group‑wise semantics across embodiments are needed. Modality gaps, false negatives, and alignment–uniformity conflicts easily arise in such settings and can prevent the latent space from becoming truly shared.

In your specific project, where egocentric video is used without proprioception or explicit kinematic models, and where locomotion behaviours must transfer across large embodiment gaps via Froude coordinates, these failure modes are especially likely. Koopman Dreamer‑style error accumulation in periodic locomotion is already challenging; adding InfoNCE’s geometric biases without tailored negative sampling or architectural constraints could exacerbate instability and make latent dynamics less transferable.

The literature does not, however, provide a direct “locomotion paper” that states, in those words, that InfoNCE is known not to transfer across bodies. Rather, the evidence is indirect: modality‑gap analyses in CLIP‑style models, false‑negative pathology studies, and successful cross‑embodiment work that uses modified InfoNCE or alternative objectives.

## Comparison with Alternative Objectives for Morphology‑Agnostic World Models

### Predictive Information Objectives vs Pure Contrastive InfoNCE

As noted earlier, predictive information objectives derived from mutual information bounds, including InfoNCE‑style variational forms, have been successfully applied to visual locomotion in PI‑ARS.[PI-ARS: Accelerating Evolution-Learned...](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13224.pdf>) These objectives focus on maximizing \(I(\text{past}; \text{future})\) in a single embodiment. In cross‑embodiment settings, one can consider extending predictive information to include multiple bodies, aiming to maximize mutual information between past and future behaviours in a morphology‑agnostic latent.

However, reliance on InfoNCE for the mutual information bound introduces the geometric issues discussed above. Alternative objectives such as JEPA‑style predictive coding and masked modelling can avoid explicit negative sampling, instead learning to predict missing or future parts of input from partial context. These objectives generally emphasize reconstruction or prediction without treating other samples as negatives, which may reduce modality or embodiment gaps but can suffer from different pathologies such as collapse or overfitting to local statistics.

JEPA‑type objectives and masked autoencoders are attractive for cross‑embodiment locomotion because they can be designed to predict Froude‑scaled body motion or egocentric visual futures directly, without forcing body‑specific samples to repel each other in latent space. In practice, combining predictive objectives with geometric regularizers, such as contractive mapping or Lipschitz constraints as in CMR,[CMR: Contractive Mapping Embeddings for...](<https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2602.03511>) may be necessary to ensure stable dynamics.

### Reconstruction + Contrastive Hybrids

The latent cross‑embodiment action‑space framework illustrates a powerful hybrid strategy: combine reconstruction loss with contrastive InfoNCE alignment, trading off alignment and self‑reconstruction via a hyperparameter \(\lambda\).[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>) The reconstruction term \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}\) ensures that each modality maintains a faithful mapping between latent and explicit actions, preserving embodiment‑specific control geometry. The contrastive term \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{contrastive}}\) ensures that semantically aligned actions across modalities map to nearby points in latent space.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>)

For cross‑embodiment locomotion, adopting a similar hybrid strategy could mitigate InfoNCE’s tendency to separate embodiments. For example, one could train encoders that map egocentric video and Froude coordinates from different bodies into a shared latent, using reconstruction or prediction losses to maintain body‑specific dynamics, while pairwise InfoNCE across retargeted Froude‑aligned sequences reinforces cross‑body alignment. Negative sampling would need to be carefully controlled to avoid cross‑embodiment false negatives, perhaps by restricting negatives to mismatched behaviours or by weighting them via Soft‑InfoNCE.[Rethinking Negative Pairs in Code Search](<https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2310.08069>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)

### Triplet Loss and Metric Learning

The cross‑embodiment humanoid motion retargeting work using triplet loss suggests that margin‑based metric learning may offer advantages in some settings.[Iii Experiments](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15419v1>) Triplet loss explicitly enforces that anchor–positive distances be smaller than anchor–negative distances by at least a margin \(\alpha\), without involving a softmax over all negatives and without temperature sensitivity.[Iii Experiments](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15419v1>) This can simplify the geometry and avoid some of the alignment–uniformity conflicts of InfoNCE, especially when latent spaces are decoupled by body segments.

For locomotion, triplet loss could be used to enforce that similar gaits across bodies lie closer in latent space than dissimilar gaits, perhaps in Froude‑scaled coordinates. However, triplet sampling is more complex than in‑batch InfoNCE and may require careful mining of positives and negatives across embodiments. It also does not directly provide a mutual information interpretation.

### Debiased and Weighted InfoNCE Variants for Cross‑Embodiment

Extensions of InfoNCE such as Soft‑InfoNCE, debiased InfoNCE, H‑InfoNCE, and adversarial variants are explicitly designed to address sampling bias, false negatives, and gradient stability.[Rethinking Negative Pairs in Code Search](<https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2310.08069>)[H-InfoNCE Loss: Enhancing Contrastive L...](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/h-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>) Soft‑InfoNCE introduces per‑negative weights in the denominator, allowing potential false negatives to be downweighted or excluded.[Rethinking Negative Pairs in Code Search](<https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2310.08069>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>) Debiased InfoNCE corrects for sampling bias in recommendation contexts, decomposing the negative expectation into mixture components to handle unlabeled positives.[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>) H‑InfoNCE and AdvInfoNCE emphasize hard negative mining and robust optimization, improving generalization and robustness to false negatives and out‑of‑distribution items.[H-InfoNCE Loss: Enhancing Contrastive L...](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/h-infonce-loss>)[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>)

These variants could, in principle, be adapted to cross‑embodiment locomotion. For example, Soft‑InfoNCE could weight cross‑embodiment negatives based on estimated semantic distance in Froude coordinates, giving near‑equivalent behaviours low weight to avoid repulsion, while giving mismatched behaviours higher weight. Mixed negative sampling as in xNCE—sampling negatives interchangeably from all embodiments rather than keeping them modality‑specific—could help reduce embodiment gaps and encourage population‑level mixing.[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>) Combining such weighted InfoNCE with reconstruction and predictive objectives might yield a morphology‑agnostic latent that is both aligned and uniform enough to support shared world models.

## Practical Implications for Your Cross‑Embodiment Locomotion Project

### Why the Informal Statement Resonates with Your Experience

Your informal statement that “InfoNCE has a specific history of not transferring across bodies, even when intended to be shared” resonates with the modality‑gap and alignment–uniformity conflict literature. These papers show that in multimodal settings, InfoNCE with independent encoders tends to produce separated distributions for different data sources, with significant modality gaps that harm group‑wise semantics and geometric structure needed for tasks like clustering and, by analogy, morphology‑agnostic locomotion.[Closing the Modality Gap Aligns Group-W...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18525v1>)[The Geometric Mechanics of Contrastive...](<https://yichaocai.com/nce_geo.github.io/>)[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>)[\[Literature Review\] Towards Uniformity...](<https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/towards-uniformity-and-alignment-for-multimodal-representation-learning>) From the perspective of your project, where you aim to use a shared latent to drive different bodies with Froude‑based coordinates, such gaps would manifest as **latent spaces that are nominally shared but practically body‑specific**, undermining cross‑embodiment policies.

At the same time, your own work with V‑JEPA‑style latent world models and FTM predictors has already demonstrated that under certain objectives, morphology‑agnostic latent dynamics are feasible: Froude coordinate transfer works, and egocentric latents contain usable signal. The bottleneck now lies in the RL optimizer and in long‑horizon error accumulation, not necessarily in latent alignment. ### Incorporating Insights from Egocentric Visual Self‑Modeling

The Egocentric Visual Self‑Modeling work by Hu, Chen, and Lipson demonstrates that a robot can learn self‑models of its own dynamics from egocentric visual input alone, enabling autonomous prediction and adaptation without explicit kinematic models. This relates strongly to your vision‑only locomotion project and reinforces the idea that **self‑modeling from egocentric views can provide a morphology‑aware yet latent‑space‑friendly world model**. While that paper does not rely primarily on InfoNCE, its results suggest that predictive latent modelling with egocentric visuals can capture the relevant dynamics without necessarily requiring strong contrastive uniformization.

For cross‑embodiment extension of egocentric self‑models, it may be beneficial to treat each body’s egocentric self‑model as a component in a larger multi‑embodiment latent, rather than to force all bodies into a single shared encoding via a symmetric InfoNCE loss. One could instead design a hierarchy of latents: local embodiment‑specific latents trained with predictive and self‑modelling objectives, and a higher‑level morphology‑agnostic latent that captures Froude‑scaled motion and other shared physical variables, with alignment enforced via carefully designed contrastive or metric learning within that shared space.

### Designing Latent Spaces and Losses for Morphology‑Agnostic Locomotion

Given the current evidence, a plausible design for your cross‑embodiment locomotion latent would include the following elements:

First, use predictive information or JEPA‑style objectives at the base level to learn robust egocentric world models for each embodiment individually, ensuring long‑horizon predictive stability and avoiding explicit negative sampling that could introduce embodiment gaps. The focus here is on learning a self‑consistent latent dynamics for each body that can support RL and control.

Second, construct a shared Froude‑space latent that encodes body‑motion coordinates across embodiments, trained with a combination of reconstruction and contrastive alignment. Contrastive terms should be carefully designed, perhaps using pairwise InfoNCE across retargeted Froude‑aligned sequences or using triplet loss with motion classes as positives and negatives. Negative sampling must avoid treating semantically similar cross‑embodiment behaviours as negatives; Soft‑InfoNCE or weighted negatives can help.

Third, consider mixed negative sampling strategies as in xNCE to reduce embodiment gaps, sampling negatives interchangeably from all embodiments and possibly including intra‑embodiment negatives to maintain within‑body class structure.[On the modality gap and the contrastive...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.10698v1>) Adjust temperature to avoid low‑temperature mode failures and monitor modality or embodiment distances in latent space during training.

Fourth, integrate geometric regularizers such as Lipschitz constraints or contractive mapping penalties to ensure that latent dynamics remain stable and that small changes in inputs produce controlled changes in outputs, as seen in CMR.[CMR: Contractive Mapping Embeddings for...](<https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2602.03511>) This can help mitigate long‑horizon error accumulation and maintain world‑model reliability.

Finally, maintain explicit monitoring of group‑wise semantics, such as latent clustering of gait types across embodiments. Despite its limitations, InfoNCE remains a powerful tool for representation learning, particularly when its design knobs—negative sampling, temperature, weighting, and architectural integration—are carefully tuned.[H-InfoNCE Loss: Enhancing Contrastive L...](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/h-infonce-loss>)[Contrastive InfoNCE Loss Overview](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/contrastive-infonce-loss>)[InfoNCE Loss Overview - Emergent Mind](<https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/infonce-loss>)[Contrastive Learning for Retrieval: Inf...](<https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/contrastive-learning-retrieval-infonce-dpr>)[Empowering Collaborative Filtering with...](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/13f1750b825659394a6499399e7637fc-Paper-Conference.pdf>) The success of latent action space alignment across robot embodiments suggests that InfoNCE can provide precisely the kind of shared latent you need, but only when combined with reconstruction losses, retargeted data, and controlled negative sampling.[Iv Experimental Results And...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v4>)[Cross-embodiment policies outperform or...](<https://x.com/gm8xx8/status/1937426495724556643>)[Latent Cross-Embodiment Policies](<https://www.emergentmind.com/articles/latent-cross-embodiment-policies>)

## Conclusion

The literature does not contain a simple sentence stating that “InfoNCE is known not to transfer across bodies,” but it does provide a coherent body of evidence showing that standard InfoNCE with independent encoders and naive negative sampling has structural tendencies that are misaligned with morphology‑agnostic and multimodal group‑wise semantics. For your cross‑embodiment locomotion project, the most accurate conclusion is that **InfoNCE is a double‑edged tool**. Going forward, you can leverage the modality‑gap insights to avoid pitfalls, borrow ideas from latent cross‑embodiment policies to design your latent action space, and integrate predictive information and egocentric self‑modelling to build robust world models. InfoNCE should be treated as a component in a larger geometric and dynamical design problem, not as a standalone solution, and the question should shift from “does InfoNCE transfer across bodies?” to “under what conditions and with what modifications can InfoNCE support morphology‑agnostic locomotion?”
