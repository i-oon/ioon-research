## 1. Project Context and the Role of Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling

### 1.1 The Cross-Embodiment Locomotion Problem

Your project’s target is unusually stringent: you want to learn a world model and latent action representation from egocentric video such that behaviour from one robot—an 18‑DOF stick‑insect in CoppeliaSim—can be used to drive a different robot—a 12‑DOF Unitree B1‑class quadruped in MuJoCo—via a shared body‑motion coordinate, specifically Froude‑scaled forward, lateral, and yaw motion.[FINDINGS.txt] You have imposed strong constraints: no kinematic models, no URDFs, no manual morphology descriptors, and no direct demonstrations on the target body. The only available signals are egocentric or allocentric camera views, joint commands, and measured body motion. The core scientific question is therefore how to learn a latent space that factors out morphology and encodes “what this action does to the body” rather than “which specific body executed it”.

Your Stage 1 pipeline already implements a sophisticated separation of roles. A large frozen video encoder based on V‑JEPA2 ViT‑g/16 is used to map images into an embedding \(e_t\), which you have shown retains morphology information: a held‑out medium‑length body lies at the correct position between long and short bodies on a morphology axis derived from these embeddings.[FINDINGS.txt] On top of this encoder, you train an intermediate latent space \(z\in\mathbb{R}^{64}\) (your ITM) and a Motion Decoder that maps \(z\) to joint commands. The world model is trained to reconstruct joint commands and predict subsequent motion, with a reconstruction loss intended to make \(z\) a latent action and a motion loss intended to constrain \(z\) in terms of dynamics. However, your measurements show that the reconstruction loss accounts for roughly 99% of the gradient flowing into \(z\) while contributing only 3–7% of the forward model’s accuracy, meaning the latent is shaped almost entirely by a tiny fraction of the signal, and the cheapest solution is to encode body identity rather than a transferable action.[FINDINGS.txt]

This leads to the failure of cross‑embodiment transfer. When tested on a held‑out medium body bracketed between long and short, the morphology signal survives the encoder but is lost in the ITM and decoder. The decoder’s output sits only half‑way toward the correct morphology axis position, yielding errors of over 11 degrees RMSE in key joints, far beyond the variance of the underlying walking signal.[FINDINGS.txt] A linear probe trained on \(z\) performs markedly better out of distribution than the decoder but still fails to recover the full morphology, confirming that the latent has the capacity but is not being used correctly. This body‑code pathology is further demonstrated by probe accuracy measurements: with five training bodies, the latent contains an 11% component that the world model can use to identify body identity, which then acts as a lookup table and fails to extend to unseen morphologies.[FINDINGS.txt]

In short, your current system has three characteristics: a powerful morphology‑aware encoder, a latent space that is underconstrained by the dynamics loss and overconstrained by reconstruction, and a decoder that learns to exploit a cheap body code rather than a morphology‑agnostic action. No decoder‑side intervention—reweighting losses, adding auxiliary heads, or inserting regularisers—has been able to fix this, because the latent itself is not constrained to be an action. What finally improves performance in your experiments is a conceptual change: decoding one body’s latent against another body’s frame, supervised by that body’s command. This cross‑decoding makes body lookup wrong by construction, forces the decoder to read morphology from the frame rather than from \(z\), and yields a 23–26% improvement in held‑out error, a dramatic increase in frame ablation sensitivity (from 10.7× to 54×), and a swap test in which predictions follow the frame to within 0.01 degrees.[FINDINGS.txt] This is promising, but it still leaves the latent under‑constrained.

It is exactly at this point that egocentric visual self‑modeling becomes relevant. Hu et al.’s work attacks a closely related problem—learning a task‑agnostic dynamic self‑model from vision alone—and solves it by placing the loss directly on future body motion predicted from egocentric video and motor commands, without reconstruction and without explicit morphology labels.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) The way they ask the question, and the way they use vision, can inform how you reshape your latent and loss.

### 1.2 Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling: Capabilities and Relevance

Hu, Chen, and Lipson’s paper “Egocentric Visual Self‑Modeling for Autonomous Robot Dynamics Prediction and Adaptation” introduces a self‑supervised learning framework in which a legged robot learns to predict its own future state from sequences of egocentric images and motor commands, without any prior knowledge of its morphology, kinematics, or task.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) Their central claim is that a dynamic self‑model can be learned using only a single first‑person camera, and that this model can support locomotion, anomaly detection, and cross‑robot generalisation. The capability you identified as most critical to your project—task‑agnostic dynamic prediction from egocentric vision across different bodies—matches what Hu et al. achieve. They explicitly aim to capture the entire dynamics of the robot using video images without any prior assumptions or knowledge of geometry, kinematics, or task.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) During training, the self‑model takes in a sequence of five egocentric images plus motor commands (current and future joint positions) and learns to predict the future state of the robot. In doing so, it implicitly learns properties such as mass distribution, geometry, friction, actuation delays, and sensing delays.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) When evaluated on locomotion tasks, legged robots equipped with this egocentric self‑model predict future position and orientation changes more accurately than robots without visual input.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) This is exactly the kind of latent you want: a morphology‑aware but task‑agnostic representation of how actions change body state, learned from vision alone.

Beyond dynamics prediction, egocentric visual self‑modeling can support damage detection and adaptation. By comparing predicted states to observed states, the robot can autonomously detect anomalies (such as bent or non‑functional legs) and adjust its gait to compensate.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>) Although you are not yet claiming damage recovery, this capability emerges from the same kind of world model: one that can simulate the robot’s body under various hypothetical actions. This aligns with your longer‑term interest in world‑model‑based control.

Hu et al. emphasise egocentric visual self‑modeling as a path toward more autonomous, adaptable, and resilient robots.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Yuhang Hu](<https://yuhang-hu.com/>)[Duke University | Discovery Robotics Re...](<https://generalroboticslab.com/papers>) They position their work within a broader trend: using vision to enable robots to perceive both their environment and their own bodies and to learn and adapt in a self‑supervised manner.[Yuhang Hu](<https://yuhang-hu.com/>)[Duke University | Discovery Robotics Re...](<https://generalroboticslab.com/papers>) This is further elaborated in their later work “Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of Themselves,” where robots watch themselves in a mirror (an external camera) and learn full‑body simulations of their morphology and kinematics from video alone.[Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12151>)[H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot](<https://github.com/H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot>) In that work, the self‑learned simulation allows accurate motion planning, detection of abnormalities, and recovery from damage, again without kinematic priors.[Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12151>) Together, these works demonstrate that your ambition—cross‑embodiment locomotion from vision without URDFs—is not only conceptually sound but directly in line with the state of the art. ### 1.3 Egocentric versus Allocentric Representations

Your project explicitly distinguishes between egocentric and allocentric camera views for manipulator and locomotion tasks. The egocentric perspective represents entities with respect to the agent’s own orientation and position, whereas the allocentric frame represents entities in an external, environment‑centred coordinate system.[Egocentric Representations for Autonomo...](<https://hal.science/tel-01684994v2/file/PhD-Thesis-Hendry_FERREIRA_CHAME_2016.pdf>) In the study of spatial cognition, this distinction is fundamental: egocentric bearings are defined relative to the organism’s heading, while allocentric bearings and positions are defined in a world coordinate frame independent of the agent.[Egocentric Representations for Autonomo...](<https://hal.science/tel-01684994v2/file/PhD-Thesis-Hendry_FERREIRA_CHAME_2016.pdf>) For humanoid robots, egocentric representations have been used to learn obstacle avoidance and motion primitives directly from first‑person camera input, with behaviour emerging as a function of embodied features and predictive models rather than explicit maps.[Egocentric Representations for Autonomo...](<https://hal.science/tel-01684994v3/file/these-utf8.pdf>)[Egocentric Representations for Autonomo...](<https://hal.science/tel-01684994v2/file/PhD-Thesis-Hendry_FERREIRA_CHAME_2016.pdf>)

Hu et al.’s egocentric visual self‑modeling adopts a purely egocentric view: the camera is mounted on the robot, and the network learns dynamics relative to the robot’s own body.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) In contrast, their “Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of Themselves” uses an allocentric “mirror” view, where the robot observes itself via an external RGB camera and learns a full‑body occupancy model and kinematics in world space.[Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12151>)[H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot](<https://github.com/H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>) Both views can support self‑modeling, but they encode different information. Egocentric vision is particularly well‑suited for learning how actions change body pose and motion because it directly captures proprioception‑like information about the robot’s heading and environment relative to the body.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>) Allocentric vision is well‑suited for learning morphology and workspace occupancy: Chen et al.’s full‑body visual self‑modeling shows that a query‑driven occupancy model can answer space‑occupancy queries conditioned on the robot’s state, enabling motion planning and control tasks.[\[PDF\] Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of...](<https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Full-Body-Visual-Self-Modeling-of-Robot-Chen-Kwiatkowski/42908b31098fd6760fc35e23e77c9c6d3b03e4f9>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>)

For your cross‑embodiment locomotion, these two views can be complementary. ## 2. Technical Dissection of Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling

### 2.1 Architecture: Visual Encoder, Action Encoder, and Recurrent Fusion

Hu et al.’s egocentric visual self‑model applies a hybrid architecture combining deep convolutional residual networks with recurrent neural networks to fuse visual and motor inputs and predict future robot states.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) The model takes two primary inputs: a sequence of five consecutive grayscale images of size \(128\times 128\) from the egocentric camera, and a vector of 12 motor angle values representing joint commands.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) These inputs are processed by two separate encoders. The visual encoder consists of 48 convolutional layers inspired by residual networks, followed by five Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) modules that embed temporal dynamics within the sequential images.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) The action encoder consists of three fully connected layers with Leaky Rectified Linear Unit (Leaky ReLU) activations; it maps the 12‑dimensional action vector into a high‑dimensional latent space.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>)

After encoding, the outputs of the visual and action encoders are concatenated and passed through five fully connected layers, each followed by a Leaky ReLU activation.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) The final output layer predicts six numbers that represent the robot’s future change in position and orientation, typically expressed as \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\), \(\Delta z\) and changes in roll, pitch, and yaw.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) Each set of motor commands is fused with five recent frames, and the network is trained to minimise the mean‑squared error between predicted and observed future state changes over a chosen prediction horizon.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) Formally, if we denote the visual input sequence as \(I_{t-4:t}\), the action vector as \(a_t\), and the future state change over horizon \(H\) as \(\Delta s_{t+H}\in\mathbb{R}^6\), the network approximates a function
\[
f_{\theta}: (I_{t-4:t}, a_t) \mapsto \Delta s_{t+H}
\]
and the loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{dyn}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t} \left\| f_\theta(I_{t-4:t}, a_t) - \Delta s_{t+H}\right\|^2.
\]
This architecture explicitly separates visual and motor encoding, then combines them for dynamics prediction. The depth of the visual encoder allows it to capture rich information about the robot’s body and environment from egocentric video, including implicit morphology encoding. The recurrent structure captures temporal dependencies, which are crucial for locomotion. The action encoder ensures that joint commands influence predictions in a high‑dimensional latent space, giving the model capacity to represent complex actuation–motion relationships.

Two features of this architecture are particularly relevant to your latent design. First, there is no reconstruction of the action from the latent; the latent is designed solely to propagate information needed for predicting future state changes. Second, morphology is not encoded explicitly—there are no labels or URDFs—but it is implicitly present in the visual features. This means that the latent representation produced by the fusion of visual and action encoders must be both morphology‑aware and dynamics‑centric, because the only way to minimise \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\) is to use morphology information extracted from vision to correctly map actions to state changes.

### 2.2 Self-Supervised Training and Domain Randomisation

The learning process for the egocentric visual self‑model is self‑supervised. The robot collects egocentric video and motor data as it moves, either through random motor babbling or through predefined gaits, and uses this data to train the network to predict its own future state.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) No manual labels are required beyond the measured pose changes, and no task‑specific rewards or objectives are used. This aligns with your desire to avoid demonstrations on the target body and to rely only on sensor data.

Hu et al. emphasise that the model can be trained purely in simulation and then transferred to the real world, thanks to extensive domain randomisation and data augmentation.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) In simulation, they vary environmental conditions (lighting, textures, obstacles), robot dynamics (mass distribution, friction coefficients, actuation noise), and sensor properties.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>) They also apply data augmentation to images, such as random cropping, blurring, and colour jitter, to prevent overfitting to specific visual conditions.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) This randomisation ensures that the learned self‑model is robust to a wide range of conditions and that it does not rely on brittle visual cues.

Domain randomisation is crucial for cross‑robot generalisation. By seeing many variations of robot bodies and environments during training, the model learns a representation that generalises across different morphologies and configurations.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Duke University | Discovery Robotics Re...](<https://generalroboticslab.com/papers>) Hu et al. validate this by applying the same self‑model across robots with different leg configurations and demonstrate that it can predict dynamics accurately and support locomotion tasks without retraining.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Duke University | Discovery Robotics Re...](<https://generalroboticslab.com/papers>) For your cross‑embodiment project, similar randomisation will be necessary: an egocentric dynamic self‑model trained on one body must see enough morphological variation to avoid encoding a simple body ID in the latent.

From a loss‑design perspective, the key point is that the self‑model is trained with a single primary objective—predicting future state changes—and that morphology generalisation emerges as a side effect of this objective coupled with visual variation. There is no reconstruction loss that could dominate gradients, and there is no explicit body‑identity supervision that could encourage body codes. The network is forced to extract the morphological features that matter for mapping actions to dynamics because those are the only features that reduce \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\).

### 2.3 Implicit Learning of Morphology, Kinematics, and Dynamics

A striking aspect of Hu et al.’s approach is that the self‑model implicitly learns morphology, kinematics, and dynamics from vision and motor data without any prior knowledge.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) As the robot observes its own movements, the network learns how joint commands affect body pose and orientation. In doing so, it encodes the robot’s geometry (limb lengths, joint ranges), mass distribution, friction with the ground, actuation delays, and sensor delays, because all of these factors influence the observed state changes.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) The model effectively builds an internal simulation of the robot’s body and its interaction with the environment.

This implicit self‑simulation capability is further elaborated in “Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of Themselves,” where robots use mirror‑like external cameras to learn full‑body occupancy models and kinematics in a differentiable form.[Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12151>)[H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot](<https://github.com/H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot>) In that work, the learned self‑simulation enables motion planning for complex tasks and supports damage detection and recovery, demonstrating that visual self‑models can go beyond simple dynamics prediction to full‑body control.[Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12151>) Chen et al.’s full‑body visual self‑modeling similarly constructs a query‑driven occupancy model that answers space‑occupancy queries conditioned on the robot’s state, which is memory‑efficient, fully differentiable, and accurate to about one percent of the workspace.[\[PDF\] Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of...](<https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Full-Body-Visual-Self-Modeling-of-Robot-Chen-Kwiatkowski/42908b31098fd6760fc35e23e77c9c6d3b03e4f9>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>) This occupancy model can be used for motion planning and control tasks, and it allows the robot to detect, localise, and recover from real‑world damage.[\[PDF\] Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of...](<https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Full-Body-Visual-Self-Modeling-of-Robot-Chen-Kwiatkowski/42908b31098fd6760fc35e23e77c9c6d3b03e4f9>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>)

These works suggest a hierarchy of self‑models. At the lowest level, egocentric dynamics models like Hu et al.’s encode local state changes for locomotion. At an intermediate level, full‑body occupancy models encode geometry and kinematics in world space. At the highest level, self‑simulation frameworks combine these to plan complex tasks. Your cross‑embodiment project sits between the lowest and intermediate levels: you need a dynamics‑centric latent for locomotion that is morphological‑agnostic enough to transfer across bodies, but you may eventually benefit from occupancy models for collision‑aware planning. The key is that all of these models rely on visual self‑supervision rather than URDFs or handwritten kinematics, which matches your constraints.

### 2.4 Relation to World-Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

Although Hu et al.’s egocentric visual self‑model is trained in a supervised, self‑supervised manner rather than via reinforcement learning, it is conceptually a world model: a learned predictor of how the environment (here, the robot’s body) evolves under actions. In world‑model‑based RL frameworks such as DreamerV3, a recurrent state‑space model (RSSM) is trained to predict observations, rewards, and discounts from latent states and actions, and an actor‑critic is trained on trajectories imagined through this model.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) DreamerV3 uses a neural network encoder to map observations to latent states \(z_t\), a dynamics predictor to propagate \(z_t\) given actions, and decoders to reconstruct observations and rewards.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) The latent space is trained to support multi‑step imagination, and the critic learns value functions over latent states, while the actor is optimized to maximise expected returns based on imagined rollouts.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>)

Koopman Dreamer extends this by imposing a spectrally structured transition on the latent dynamics, controlling the spectral radius of the transition operator to bound error accumulation over long horizons.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>) By ensuring that latent modes have bounded persistence, Koopman Dreamer balances expressiveness against stability and improves long‑horizon prediction and closed‑loop control on continuous‑control tasks including locomotion and UAV navigation.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>) AdaMVE, on the other hand, addresses compounding error by learning state‑dependent estimates of cumulative model error and adaptively choosing planning horizons based on these estimates, so that the agent trusts the model only over horizons where error remains tolerable.[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>)

These RL frameworks differ from Hu et al.’s self‑model primarily in their objectives: they optimise latent dynamics to support reward maximisation rather than pure dynamics prediction. However, they share a reliance on accurate multi‑step prediction and on controlling compounding error. For your project, the egocentric visual self‑model can serve as the dynamics backbone for a world‑model‑based RL controller. You can train the self‑model as Hu et al. do, then plug it into a Dreamer‑style RL loop with spectral constraints or adaptive horizon selection. This would allow you to treat the latent as a morphology‑agnostic body‑motion coordinate and to use RL to learn policies that transfer across bodies, while keeping model usage under control.

## 3. Comparing Your Pipeline to Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling

### 3.1 Encoder-Level Morphology Representation

Your Stage 1 findings demonstrate that the frozen V‑JEPA2 ViT‑g/16 encoder already captures morphology information in a linearly decodable form that generalises to unseen bodies.[FINDINGS.txt] Specifically, when measuring the held‑out medium body’s position on a morphology axis between long and short bodies, defined in embedding space and joint space, you found that the frozen encoder’s embedding \(e_t\) places the medium body at axis position 0.499 (approximately the expected 0.5 based on leg scale alone), and 0.465 when using fewer clips.[FINDINGS.txt] This indicates that the encoder has learned to represent the robot’s body shape and leg lengths in a way that is robust across training samples. When the axis is defined in joint space, the correct positions for the medium body are 0.357 for the CF joint and 0.304 for the FT joint, with the encoder preserving these positions within small deviations.[FINDINGS.txt]

In other words, your encoder is doing exactly what Chen et al.’s full‑body visual self‑modeling suggests is possible: encoding morphology in a way that supports query‑based reasoning and generalisation.[\[PDF\] Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of...](<https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Full-Body-Visual-Self-Modeling-of-Robot-Chen-Kwiatkowski/42908b31098fd6760fc35e23e77c9c6d3b03e4f9>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>) The problem lies not in the encoder but downstream. Hu et al.’s visual encoder also captures morphology implicitly, because egocentric images contain consistent information about limb lengths, joint placements, and body shape.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) However, their pipeline preserves this morphology signal all the way to the output because the loss demands correct mapping from actions to state changes, and the model cannot satisfy this without using morphology information. In your case, the loss geometry does not demand such usage.

A key difference is that Hu et al. do not freeze their visual encoder; it is trained jointly with the rest of the network. This allows morphology features to be sculpted into forms that are useful for dynamics prediction. In your pipeline, freezing the encoder stabilises representation but may also limit how morphology features can be repurposed downstream. Given that you have already verified that the frozen encoder’s morphology representation generalises well, this freezing is not inherently problematic. However, it emphasises the importance of properly using the encoder’s features in the latent \(z\) rather than re‑encoding morphology from scratch.

### 3.2 Latent Action Versus Body Code

The central pathology in your pipeline is that the latent \(z\) is not acting as an action; instead, it acts as a cheap body code. Your reconstruction loss, which is intended to make \(z\) a latent action by reconstructing joint commands, contributes only 3–7% of the forward model’s accuracy yet takes 99% of the gradient.[FINDINGS.txt] This means that the gradient flowing into \(z\) is dominated by impulses that encourage reconstruction of current joint commands rather than accurate prediction of future motion. Because the motion loss is underweighted, the network can minimise total loss by encoding body identity in \(z\), allowing the decoder to reconstruct joint commands using a body‑specific lookup plus phase information extracted from the frame.

Your measurements confirm this. When applying a ridge regression probe from \(z\) to joint commands, trained on the two training bodies and tested on the held‑out body, you found that the probe’s reconstruction error on training bodies was higher than the decoder’s (3.56 versus 0.63 degrees RMSE on long; 3.79 versus 0.92 degrees on short) but that the probe degraded only 1.4× on the held‑out body, while the decoder degraded 15× (5.13 versus 11.04 degrees RMSE on medium).[FINDINGS.txt] The probe’s axis position for the held‑out body (0.335) matched that of \(z\) (0.3352), whereas the decoder’s axis position (0.188) was far off the correct 0.357.[FINDINGS.txt] This shows that the decoder is using \(z\) in a highly non‑linear way to encode a body‑specific mapping that does not generalise to unseen morphologies, while the linear probe reflects the underlying morphology information buried in \(z\).

This contrasts sharply with Hu et al.’s latent usage. In their architecture, the latent is built from the fusion of visual and action encoders and is used solely to predict future body‑state changes.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) There is no reconstruction of actions, and thus no incentive to encode body identity in the latent beyond what is necessary for dynamics prediction. The loss forces the latent to represent “what this action does to this body,” not “which body is currently acting.” As a result, the latent can generalise across bodies as long as morphology variation is present in the visual input and the model has seen enough examples.

Your cross‑decoding intervention (F24) begins to enforce a similar discipline. By decoding one body’s latent against another body’s frame and supervising using the second body’s command, you construct a training scenario where the decoder cannot rely on latent body codes, because the latent and frame refer to different bodies.[FINDINGS.txt] The decoder is forced to read morphology from the frame and use the latent as an action descriptor. This leads to improved held‑out error and increased frame sensitivity, indicating that the decoder is now correctly using the frame for morphology and the latent for action. However, the latent itself remains shaped primarily by reconstruction, because the loss still penalises errors in reconstructing joint commands. The next step is to push your objective closer to \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\) and reduce or restructure reconstruction.

### 3.3 Loss Geometry and Gradient Imbalance

The gradient imbalance you report—reconstruction loss occupying 99% of the gradient while contributing only a small fraction of motion accuracy—is a textbook example of a misaligned objective. In multi‑task learning, when losses are combined as \(\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{rec}\mathcal{L}_{rec} + \lambda_{dyn}\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\), the effective gradient on shared parameters such as \(z\) is a linear combination of gradients from each task weighted by \(\lambda\). If \(\lambda_{rec}\gg \lambda_{dyn}\), the gradient direction will be dominated by \(\nabla\mathcal{L}_{rec}\), and the network will prioritise reconstructing actions over predicting dynamics, even if \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\) is more important for the end task.

In your case, this imbalance leads to an under‑constrained latent. The motion loss shapes \(z\) only through one percent of the gradient, and there is no additional structure (such as orthogonality constraints, disentangling losses, or information bottlenecks) to force \(z\) to represent transferable actions. As a result, encoding body identity becomes the cheapest way to satisfy the motion loss: the decoder can learn a body‑specific mapping between \(z\) and joint commands that fits training bodies well but fails on new morphologies.

Hu et al.’s self‑model avoids this by having a single primary objective \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\). There is no reconstruction loss and thus no competition for gradient. The latent is fully constrained by the need to predict dynamics accurately. Other world‑model frameworks such as DreamerV3 also pay careful attention to loss geometry, using separate losses for observation reconstruction, reward prediction, and continuation, and sometimes balancing them with techniques like stop‑gradient, symlog normalisation, and careful weighting.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) Koopman Dreamer further constrains latent dynamics by bounding the spectral radius of the transition operator, stabilising long‑horizon prediction.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>)

Your cross‑decoding approach hints at a more sophisticated geometry: it implicitly introduces a disentangling between morphology and action by forcing the decoder to read morphology from the frame. To push this further, you can explicitly design a latent structure with two components, \(z = [z_{act}, z_{id}]\), and then impose constraints such that \(z_{id}\) does not influence reconstruction or dynamics prediction, or that its information content is minimised. For example, an information bottleneck loss or adversarial training could be used to drive mutual information \(I(z_{act}; \text{body ID})\) toward zero while maintaining high \(I(z_{act}; \text{body motion})\). Another approach is to drop reconstruction entirely and treat joint commands as purely inputs to the self‑model, as Hu et al. do.

### 3.4 Egocentric Versus Allocentric Frames in Your Data

Your Stage 1 experiments used data from two datasets: an allocentric framed dataset (ik_walk_100_framed) with two training bodies differing in one parameter and a longer allocentric dataset with five bodies differing in three parameters.[FINDINGS.txt] These datasets provided external views of the stick‑insect robot walking, with expert gaits. The encoder extracts morphology from these allocentric views, as evidenced by the morphology axis analysis. However, your longer‑term plan is to work with egocentric views, especially for the quadruped.

Egocentric views differ from allocentric views in that they directly capture the robot’s heading and motion relative to the environment, which is crucial for locomotion. In Ferreira Chame’s work on egocentric representations for humanoid navigation, egocentric features were defined in terms of body‑centred bearings and distances, and predictive models were used to anticipate context and relate actual measurements to anticipated outcomes, enabling obstacle avoidance and motion primitives.[Egocentric Representations for Autonomo...](<https://hal.science/tel-01684994v3/file/these-utf8.pdf>)[Egocentric Representations for Autonomo...](<https://hal.science/tel-01684994v2/file/PhD-Thesis-Hendry_FERREIRA_CHAME_2016.pdf>) For locomotion, egocentric vision can encode periodic patterns in leg motion and contact, similar to the gait cycles Hu et al. model.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>)

Practically, this means that your encoder’s morphology representation learned from allocentric views may not directly transfer to egocentric views without retraining or adaptation. However, you can use the insights from Stage 1 to design an egocentric encoder that preserves morphology and phase information, and then apply the egocentric dynamic self‑modeling loss to shape the latent. The cross‑decoding idea can be extended to egocentric frames by pairing latents from one time step with frames from another time step or from another body, supervised by the appropriate actions. This would force the decoder to read morphology and phase from egocentric frames rather than from \(z\), and encourage \(z\) to encode body‑motion coordinates.

## 4. Long-Horizon Error, World-Model RL, and Model Trust

### 4.1 Compounding Error and State-Dependent Planning Horizons

World‑model‑based RL offers a powerful paradigm for planning and control, but it suffers from the compounding‑error problem: small one‑step prediction errors accumulate over multiple steps, distorting imagined trajectories and leading to poor policy updates.[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>)[When to Trust Your Model: Model-Based P...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253>) Ideally, an agent would trust its world model over horizons where errors remain bounded and switch to model‑free updates or shorter rollouts when errors grow large. AdaMVE, introduced by Chenjun Xiao and colleagues, tackles this by learning state‑dependent estimates of cumulative model error and choosing planning horizons accordingly.[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>)

AdaMVE decomposes cumulative model errors temporally, learning a function \(\hat{E}(s,a,h)\) that approximates the maximum cumulative model error around state \(s\) and action \(a\) over horizon \(h\).[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>) The algorithm applies Temporal Difference methods to learn these model errors and defines a planning horizon \(H(s)\) for each state based on the learned error estimates. At states where the model is accurate, a longer horizon is used to exploit the model’s advantages; at states where the model errors are large, a shorter horizon is adopted to reduce compounding error.[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>) Experimental results show that AdaMVE can significantly alleviate compounding error, improving policy learning efficiency compared to both pure model‑based and pure model‑free baselines.[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>)

Janner et al.’s MBPO similarly emphasises the importance of short model‑generated rollouts branched from real data.[When to Trust Your Model: Model-Based P...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253>) They show that using short model rollouts, combined with off‑policy real data, can achieve the sample efficiency of model‑based methods without the usual pitfalls associated with long rollouts, and can match the asymptotic performance of state‑of‑the‑art model‑free algorithms.[When to Trust Your Model: Model-Based P...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253>) The key insight is that model usage must be carefully controlled: real data is always preferable when the model generalisation is poor, and model rollouts are beneficial only when the model is accurate.

For your project, compounding error is a concern because your world model must extrapolate across morphologies and over multiple steps of locomotion. If the latent dynamics are poorly constrained or if morphology generalisation fails, long‑horizon rollouts will be systematically biased, leading to divergent critic estimates and unstable actor updates. Adopting a state‑dependent horizon selection mechanism such as AdaMVE can prevent your RL loop from trusting the world model at states and horizons where morphology generalisation is weak. You can measure cumulative prediction error of your egocentric self‑model across horizons and use that to bound imagination length.

### 4.2 Spectrally Constrained Latent Dynamics: Koopman Dreamer

Koopman Dreamer addresses long‑horizon error from another angle: by constraining the spectral properties of latent dynamics.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>) In Dreamer‑style controllers, the latent transition function propagates latent states forward under actions, and one‑step errors in this transition accumulate over multiple steps. Koopman Dreamer replaces the conventional deterministic latent transition with a spectrally structured transition whose modal persistence is explicitly bounded.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>) This is inspired by Koopman operator theory, where dynamics are represented as linear evolution in the space of observables. By constraining the spectral radius \(\kappa_\star\) of the learned transition operator, Koopman Dreamer ensures that errors remain bounded over long horizons.

The error propagation analysis in Koopman Dreamer shows that for \(\kappa_{\star}<1\), initial errors decay and accumulated residuals remain bounded by \(\bar{\delta}/(1-\kappa_{\star})\); at \(\kappa_{\star}=1\), residuals grow linearly; and for \(\kappa_{\star}>1\), errors can grow geometrically.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>) This stability–expressiveness trade‑off explains how controlling latent modal persistence reduces compounding error while preserving persistent dynamics such as oscillations in locomotion.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>) Experiments on DeepMind Control Suite tasks and UAV‑LiDAR navigation show that Koopman Dreamer achieves lower long‑horizon prediction error and better control performance than DreamerV3 on most tasks, particularly those with smooth, near‑periodic dynamics.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>)

This is directly relevant to your locomotion tasks, which are near‑periodic. If you adopt a Dreamer‑style RL loop around your egocentric self‑model, structuring latent dynamics with Koopman constraints can help maintain accurate gait cycles in imagination and avoid drift. The latent representation used by Koopman Dreamer is deterministic, and the transition function is designed to respect spectral bounds. Combining this with your morphology‑agnostic body‑motion coordinate would yield a stable latent dynamics \(z_{t+1} = g(z_t, a_t)\) whose modes are calibrated to preserve periodic locomotion without explosive error growth.

### 4.3 DreamerV3 and Critic Stability

DreamerV3 is another milestone in world‑model‑based RL that emphasises critic stability and normalisation across diverse domains.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) The architecture builds on DreamerV2, using an RSSM that predicts latent states \(z_t\) from previous states and actions, and reconstructs observations, rewards, and discounts.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) DreamerV3 focuses on a “bag of tricks” to stabilise learning: symlog normalisation of rewards, appropriately scaled replay, regularised latent dynamics, and robust critic training over imagined rollouts.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) The idea is to maintain a critic that does not diverge when trained on model‑generated trajectories, even when the model is imperfect.

The relevance to your project lies in how DreamerV3 balances observation reconstruction and reward prediction in the loss. Unlike your pipeline, where reconstruction loss dominates, DreamerV3 carefully tunes loss weights and normalisation to ensure that rewards and dynamics shape the latent as needed for control.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) You can borrow these ideas when designing your losses: ensure that dynamics prediction losses have strong influence on latent shaping, and use normalisation to prevent some losses from dominating gradients. Additionally, DreamerV3’s practice of training the world model and actor‑critic in alternating phases, with large replay buffers and consistent hyperparameters, can help avoid training instabilities when integrating your egocentric self‑model into an RL loop.

### 4.4 Robotic World Models and PPO Integration

Recent discussions of “Physical AI” and robotic world models propose using self‑supervised world models as fast simulators for RL, with PPO or other policy optimisation algorithms.[Physical AIに向けて：Robotic World Model (RWM)](<https://zenn.dev/cybernetics/articles/2bad5339dd9508>) The Robotic World Model (RWM) concept combines a dual auto‑regressive mechanism with self‑supervised learning to achieve accurate long‑term dynamics prediction without domain knowledge about specific robots or tasks.[Physical AIに向けて：Robotic World Model (RWM)](<https://zenn.dev/cybernetics/articles/2bad5339dd9508>) The RWM can be used as a differentiable simulator, enabling RL algorithms like PPO to optimise policies faster than with traditional physics simulators.[Physical AIに向けて：Robotic World Model (RWM)](<https://zenn.dev/cybernetics/articles/2bad5339dd9508>) This suggests a path where your egocentric self‑model, once trained, can replace or augment MuJoCo and CoppeliaSim for training cross‑embodiment policies, at least for certain horizons.

The key is to treat the self‑model as a learned environment and to integrate RL carefully to avoid compounding error and gradient pathologies. PPO uses on‑policy updates with clipped objective functions to stabilise policy learning, which may be less sensitive to moderate model inaccuracies than off‑policy methods. However, PPO still requires trust in the dynamics model used during rollouts. Combining PPO with short model rollouts, adaptive horizons, or spectral constraints can mitigate this risk.

## 5. Integration Blueprint: From Egocentric Self-Model to Cross-Embodiment Latent Action

### 5.1 Build an Egocentric Dynamic Self-Model Per Body

The first concrete step inspired by Hu et al. is to build an egocentric dynamic self‑model for each robot body—your stick‑insect and your quadruped—that directly maps egocentric video and joint commands to future body‑motion in a shared coordinate system. For each robot, mount a camera to provide egocentric images \(I_{t-4:t}\) and measure body pose changes \(\Delta s_{t+H}\) over a horizon \(H\), expressed in terms of forward, lateral, and yaw displacement or velocity. For locomotion, expressing state changes as Froude‑scaled velocities, such as
\[
v_{Froude} = \frac{v}{\sqrt{g l}},
\]
where \(v\) is the forward velocity, \(g\) gravity, and \(l\) a characteristic leg length, allows you to normalise motion across morphologies.[FINDINGS context] Define a shared body‑motion coordinate \(\Delta s_{t+H}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) with components corresponding to Froude‑scaled forward, lateral, and yaw motion.

For each robot \(R\), train a self‑model \(f^{(R)}_\theta\) with architecture similar to Hu et al.’s: a deep visual encoder, an action encoder, and a fusion network that predicts \(\Delta s_{t+H}\). The loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{dyn}^{(R)}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t} \left\| f^{(R)}_\theta(I^{(R)}_{t-4:t}, a^{(R)}_t) - \Delta s^{(R)}_{t+H}\right\|^2,
\]
with no reconstruction of joint commands. Using domain randomisation and data augmentation similar to Hu et al.’s, vary environmental and robot parameters during training to ensure that the model does not overfit to a fixed morphology or gait.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) For the stick‑insect, randomise body segment scales within a range while preserving overall kinematics, to encourage the model to learn a morphology‑agnostic mapping from joint commands to Froude‑scaled motion. For the quadruped, randomise payload, friction, and terrain.

Once trained, these self‑models provide a mapping from joint commands to shared body‑motion coordinates. The latent representation at the fusion layer can be interpreted as an action embedding that encodes “what this action does” in terms of body motion, conditioned on visual context. This latent is exactly what you need for cross‑embodiment transfer: the stick‑insect’s behaviours can be mapped into latent body‑motion coordinates and then fed into the quadruped’s self‑model to find joint commands that produce similar Froude‑scaled motion.

### 5.2 Define a Morphology-Agnostic Latent Action Space

To make the latent action space explicitly morphology‑agnostic, define a latent variable \(z_{act}\) at the output of the fused encoder that feeds into a small prediction head for \(\Delta s_{t+H}\). In Hu et al.’s architecture, the fused representation before the final fully connected layers can serve this role.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) For both robots, constrain the dimensionality of \(z_{act}\) to be moderate (e.g. 16–32), and train both self‑models to use the same latent dimensionality and coordinate system. You can implement this by sharing the action encoder and the final prediction head across robots, while allowing the visual encoder to be robot‑specific, or by training a joint self‑model on multi‑robot data with robot‑specific visual encoders and a shared fusion network.

The shared latent \(z_{act}\) is trained to be predictive of \(\Delta s_{t+H}\) across robots. Because the loss is defined in a common coordinate system (Froude‑scaled motion), the latent necessarily encodes action effects in a way that can transfer between bodies. Morphology information is captured in visual features and influences how \(z_{act}\) is used to produce motion, but \(z_{act}\) itself must be interpretable in terms of body motion.

This design parallels Hu et al.’s cross‑robot generalisation experiments, where the same self‑model is applied across robots with different configurations and is shown to predict dynamics accurately.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Duke University | Discovery Robotics Re...](<https://generalroboticslab.com/papers>) It also echoes your Stage 1 finding that the frozen encoder’s morphology representation is consistent across bodies. By defining \(z_{act}\) in terms of shared body‑motion coordinates, you encourage the latent to represent action effects. You can further regularise \(z_{act}\) using an information bottleneck or a contrastive loss that encourages similar actions (in terms of \(\Delta s_{t+H}\)) to have similar latents across robots.

### 5.3 Rebalancing Losses: Reconstruction as Auxiliary or Separate

A crucial change relative to your current pipeline is to treat reconstruction of joint commands as auxiliary rather than primary. Instead of using reconstruction loss to define latent actions, use dynamics loss \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\) as the main objective and optionally add a small reconstruction term for calibration. For example, define
\[
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{dyn} + \lambda_{rec}\mathcal{L}_{rec},
\]
where
\[
\mathcal{L}_{rec} = \mathbb{E}_t \left\| g_\phi(z_{act}) - a_t\right\|^2,
\]
and choose \(\lambda_{rec}\ll 1\), e.g. \(\lambda_{rec}=0.01\), so that reconstruction does not dominate gradients. You can also apply stop‑gradient operations to prevent \(\mathcal{L}_{rec}\) from shaping the visual encoder or action encoder, treating reconstruction as a separate head that reads \(z_{act}\) without influencing its formation.

Alternatively, separate reconstruction entirely from the dynamics‑centric latent. Train a joint encoder to produce two latents, \(z_{act}\) and \(z_{rec}\), from the same inputs. Use \(z_{act}\) exclusively for dynamics prediction and cross‑embodiment transfer, with \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\) as its only loss. Use \(z_{rec}\) exclusively for reconstructing joint commands, with \(\mathcal{L}_{rec}\) applied only to \(z_{rec}\). Enforce orthogonality or low mutual information between \(z_{act}\) and \(z_{rec}\) to prevent leakage. This disentangling ensures that reconstruction does not hijack the dynamics latent.

Your cross‑decoding intervention can be folded into this. For example, during training, sample a pair of bodies \(R_i\) and \(R_j\). Encode visual frames \(I^{(R_i)}_{t-4:t}\) and actions \(a^{(R_i)}_t\) to obtain \(z^{(R_i)}_{act}\). Decode \(z^{(R_i)}_{act}\) together with frames \(I^{(R_j)}_{t-4:t}\) to predict \(\Delta s^{(R_j)}_{t+H}\), supervised by the actual motion of \(R_j\) under actions \(a^{(R_j)}_t\). This cross‑decoding forces the decoder to read morphology exclusively from frames and action effects from \(z_{act}\), generalising your F24 experiment to dynamics. The loss becomes
\[
\mathcal{L}_{cross} = \mathbb{E}_{i\neq j,t} \left\| f_\theta(I^{(R_j)}_{t-4:t}, z^{(R_i)}_{act}) - \Delta s^{(R_j)}_{t+H}\right\|^2.
\]
Combining \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{cross}\) can strengthen morphology‑agnostic latent shaping.

### 5.4 Integrate World-Model RL with Model Trust Mechanisms

Once you have a morphology‑agnostic latent action \(z_{act}\) and dynamics‑accurate self‑models, you can integrate them into a world‑model‑based RL loop to learn control policies that transfer across bodies. For each robot, treat the egocentric self‑model as the dynamics model in a Dreamer‑style controller. Learn an actor \(\pi(z)\) that outputs actions \(a_t\) based on latent states and a critic \(V(z)\) that estimates value. Train the actor‑critic on trajectories imagined within the latent dynamics, using rewards defined in terms of Froude‑scaled motion (e.g. forward velocity, energy efficiency, stability).

To avoid compounding error and critic divergence, adopt model trust mechanisms from AdaMVE, MBPO, and Koopman Dreamer. Learn state‑dependent cumulative error estimates \(\hat{E}(z,a,h)\) for your self‑model by comparing predicted and actual \(\Delta s_{t+H}\) over horizons \(h\).[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>) Use these estimates to choose planning horizons \(H(z)\): when \(\hat{E}\) is low, allow longer rollouts; when it is high, restrict to short rollouts or rely on model‑free updates. Combine imagined rollouts with short real‑data rollouts branched from actual trajectories, as MBPO recommends, to preserve grounding.[When to Trust Your Model: Model-Based P...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253>)

If you adopt Koopman Dreamer, parameterise the latent dynamics transition \(g(z,a)\) as a spectrally constrained operator with bounded spectral radius \(\kappa_\star\).[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>) For locomotion, calibrate \(\kappa_\star\) to balance persistent oscillations with error decay. For example, choose \(\kappa_\star\) slightly less than 1 to allow near‑periodic dynamics while ensuring that small errors do not explode. Train the self‑model with spectral regularisation alongside \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\). This way, imagined gait cycles remain stable over longer horizons.

Finally, borrow critic‑stability techniques from DreamerV3, such as reward symlog normalisation and stop‑gradient in certain paths, to avoid critic drift when training on model‑generated data.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) Ensure that your loss weighting does not reintroduce the kind of gradient imbalance you observed with reconstruction. Where possible, treat observation reconstruction as auxiliary and focus critic training on rewards and dynamics consistency.

### 5.5 Egocentric and Allocentric Cameras for Manipulator and Locomotion

Your interest in egocentric versus allocentric camera views extends beyond locomotion to manipulator tasks. Egocentric cameras are natural for locomotion because they align the representation with the body’s heading and the environment. Allocentric cameras are useful for manipulators and full‑body motion planning, where precise spatial relationships between the robot and objects matter. Based on Hu et al.’s and Chen et al.’s work, you can design a dual‑view system where egocentric cameras feed dynamic self‑models and allocentric cameras feed occupancy‑based morphology models.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>)

For locomotion, the egocentric self‑model predicts Froude‑scaled motion based on egocentric frames and actions. For manipulation, an allocentric self‑model could be trained to predict object motion and contact forces based on external views and manipulator joint commands, using a similar self‑supervised dynamic objective. The latent representations from these two models can be aligned by sharing some components or by training a joint model that uses both views. This would allow behaviours learned in one modality (e.g. locomotion) to inform another (e.g. reaching), and vice versa.

In full‑body tasks, the SelfSimRobot framework demonstrates that robots can learn their physical form and movement autonomously through vision alone, using a single RGB camera to capture raw video and constructing a self‑model that predicts 3D occupancy and supports motion planning.[H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot](<https://github.com/H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>) Combining this with your egocentric dynamics models could yield a rich, multi‑view world model that supports both cross‑embodiment locomotion and general manipulation without URDFs.

## 6. Experimental Designs and Actionable Next Steps

### 6.1 Diagnose and Fix Latent Loss Pathologies

The first experimental priority is to systematically diagnose and fix the latent loss pathology revealed in Stage 1. Your findings show that reconstruction loss occupies 99% of the gradient on \(z\) while contributing little to motion accuracy.[FINDINGS.txt] To confirm and refine this, instrument your training pipeline to measure gradient norms from each loss component flowing into \(z\) over time. Compute
\[
G_{rec} = \|\nabla_z \mathcal{L}_{rec}\|,\quad G_{dyn} = \|\nabla_z \mathcal{L}_{dyn}\|,
\]
and track the ratio \(G_{rec}/G_{dyn}\). If this ratio remains above, say, 50 across epochs, it confirms that reconstruction dominates shaping.

Next, progressively reduce \(\lambda_{rec}\) and observe how this ratio changes. At each \(\lambda_{rec}\), measure held‑out morphology axis positions, joint RMSE, and motion prediction accuracy. Evaluate whether reducing reconstruction weighting improves morphology generalisation and motion prediction without destabilising training. Include cross‑decoding loss \(\mathcal{L}_{cross}\) as described earlier and inspect how gradient contributions from \(\mathcal{L}_{cross}\) affect \(z\).

In parallel, implement the disentangled latent approach: separate \(z_{act}\) and \(z_{rec}\) and apply losses exclusively to their respective heads. Use mutual information estimators or adversarial discrimination to minimise \(I(z_{act}; \text{body ID})\) while preserving \(I(z_{act}; \text{body motion})\). For example, train a discriminator \(D\) to predict body ID from \(z_{act}\), and add an adversarial loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_{adv} = -\mathbb{E}[\log D(z_{act}, \text{body ID})],
\]
so that the encoder learns to fool the discriminator, reducing body ID information in \(z_{act}\). Measure whether this reduces body code usage.

Combining these diagnostics with your existing cross‑decoding experiment will help quantify how much of the pathology is due to loss weighting versus architecture. The goal is to converge on a loss geometry in which dynamics prediction dominates latent shaping and reconstruction acts only as a small regulariser or separate task.

### 6.2 Implement Egocentric Self-Models and Shared Latent Actions

The next experimental phase is to implement egocentric self‑models for both robots and define a shared latent action space. For the stick‑insect in CoppeliaSim, mount an egocentric camera and collect trajectories comprising egocentric images, joint commands, and body pose changes. For the quadruped in MuJoCo, do the same. Use the architecture from Hu et al. (48‑layer residual visual encoder, LSTM temporal modules, three‑layer action encoder, fusion MLP) as a baseline.[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) Implement the network in your preferred framework, using similar image sizes and sequence lengths.

Train individual self‑models for each robot with \(\mathcal{L}_{dyn}\) as the primary loss and domain randomisation on environments and robot parameters.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) Validate dynamics prediction accuracy by comparing predicted and actual \(\Delta s_{t+H}\) on held‑out trajectories and measuring mean‑squared error and correlation. Ensure that the models can predict forward, lateral, and yaw motion accurately over horizons spanning multiple gait cycles.

Once individual self‑models perform adequately, train a joint self‑model on data from both robots with a shared latent \(z_{act}\). This can be done by sharing the action encoder and fusion network across robots while allowing robot‑specific visual encoders. Use a joint loss that sums dynamics prediction errors across robots:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{joint} = \sum_{R} \mathcal{L}_{dyn}^{(R)}.
\]
Apply domain randomisation across robots to encourage generalisation.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Duke University | Discovery Robotics Re...](<https://generalroboticslab.com/papers>) Evaluate whether \(z_{act}\) behaves similarly for equivalent actions across robots by comparing latents for trajectories that produce similar Froude‑scaled motion. Use dimensionality‑reduction techniques to visualise \(z_{act}\) clusters and check whether locomotion behaviours align across robots.

As a cross‑embodiment test, record a gait from the stick‑insect and map its joint commands to \(z_{act}\) via the stick‑insect’s self‑model. Then feed \(z_{act}\) into the quadruped’s self‑model to derive joint commands that produce similar body‑motion coordinates. Execute these commands in MuJoCo and measure the quadruped’s motion in Froude‑scaled coordinates. Compare it to the stick‑insect’s motion; measure how closely the quadruped reproduces the overall forward, lateral, and yaw behaviour.

### 6.3 Integrate Model-Based RL with Model Trust Mechanisms

With self‑models and latent actions in place, integrate world‑model‑based RL. For each robot, treat the self‑model as the environment dynamics model. Implement a Dreamer‑style controller where the latent state comprises \(z_{act}\) and additional context features, and where the actor outputs joint commands and the critic estimates value.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) Define rewards that reflect locomotion goals, such as forward speed, energy efficiency, and stability.

To control compounding error, implement AdaMVE’s state‑dependent horizon selection. During training, estimate cumulative prediction error \(\hat{E}(z,a,h)\) for each latent state and action by comparing self‑model predictions with actual body‑motion over horizon \(h\).[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>) Use these estimates to choose planning horizons \(H(z)\) in imagination: longer horizons where \(\hat{E}\) is small, shorter horizons where \(\hat{E}\) is large. Combine imagined rollouts with short real‑data rollouts branched from real trajectories, as MBPO recommends, to ground the critic.[When to Trust Your Model: Model-Based P...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253>)

Experiment with Koopman Dreamer’s spectral constraints on latent dynamics. Parameterise the latent transition function as a linear operator plus non‑linear residual and regularise the spectral radius \(\kappa_\star\) to be less than 1.[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>) Measure how different \(\kappa_\star\) values affect long‑horizon gait prediction and control performance. Use locomotion tasks such as walking, turning, and obstacle avoidance in both simulation environments.

Monitor critic stability by tracking value estimates over training and checking for divergence. Apply DreamerV3’s techniques, such as symlog reward normalisation and hard‑target updates, to stabilise critic training.[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>) Evaluate policies learned with world‑model‑based RL on both robots and assess cross‑embodiment transfer: can a policy learned on the stick‑insect in terms of \(z_{act}\) be applied to the quadruped with minimal fine‑tuning?

### 6.4 Egocentric versus Allocentric Manipulator Experiments

Extend the self‑modeling framework to manipulator tasks with both egocentric and allocentric cameras. For example, mount a wrist‑camera on a manipulator for egocentric views and an external camera for allocentric views. Train an egocentric dynamic self‑model that predicts local end‑effector motion from egocentric images and joint commands, and an allocentric self‑model that predicts object motion and contact from external views. Use a shared latent action \(z_{act}\) defined in terms of end‑effector motion in a body‑centred coordinate system.

Evaluate how well \(z_{act}\) transfers between egocentric and allocentric views by training policies in one view and applying them in the other. For example, learn a reaching policy using allocentric views and then execute it based on egocentric latent predictions. Use SelfSimRobot’s occupancy modeling to provide a geometry prior: train an occupancy‑based self‑model that predicts 3D occupancy given actions and visual input, and use its gradients for motion planning.[H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot](<https://github.com/H-Y-H-Y-H/SelfSimRobot>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>) Combine this with your locomotion self‑models to create full‑body behaviours that involve both walking and manipulation.

These experiments will explore how egocentric and allocentric representations can be integrated into a unified world model that supports cross‑embodiment tasks beyond locomotion.

## 7. Synthesis and Recommendations

### 7.1 How Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling Relates to Your Project

You asked whether I had “forgotten” Egocentric Visual Self‑Modeling and whether it relates to your project. It does not simply relate; it sits almost exactly on top of your problem, but with one critical shift: Hu et al. asked the question “How does my body move under this action, as seen by my own camera?” and placed the loss on future body‑state changes, whereas your Stage 1 pipeline implicitly asked “Which body am I, and which joint command was just executed?” and placed most of the gradient on reconstructing actions. Hu et al. showed that an egocentric visual self‑model can be learned in a self‑supervised, task‑agnostic manner from a single first‑person camera, without morphology or kinematic priors, and that this model can predict dynamics accurately, detect damage, and transfer across robots.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) Chen et al. and Hu et al. later extended this to full‑body occupancy modeling and self‑simulation from mirror‑like external views.[\[PDF\] Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of...](<https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Full-Body-Visual-Self-Modeling-of-Robot-Chen-Kwiatkowski/42908b31098fd6760fc35e23e77c9c6d3b03e4f9>)[Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12151>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>) These capabilities—vision‑only morphology and dynamics learning, cross‑robot generalisation, and self‑simulation—are precisely what your cross‑embodiment locomotion project needs.

Your Stage 1 findings already show that your frozen encoder captures morphology in a generalisable way, and that cross‑decoding can force the decoder to read morphology from frames rather than from the latent.[FINDINGS.txt] The missing piece is a latent action representation shaped by dynamics prediction rather than reconstruction, with loss geometry that prevents cheap body codes. Egocentric visual self‑modeling provides a template for such a latent: fuse visual and action encoders, predict future body‑motion in a shared coordinate system, and drop reconstruction. World‑model‑based RL frameworks provide tools for controlling compounding error and critic stability when using such a model for planning and control.[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>)[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>)[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>)[When to Trust Your Model: Model-Based P...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253>)

In other words, Egocentric Visual Self‑Modeling is not an optional reference; it is the blueprint for reshaping your latent and loss. And your Stage 1 cross‑decoding results show that you are already moving in that direction by changing the question.

## 8. Conclusion

Your Stage 1 findings identify a subtle but decisive failure mode in your current world‑model architecture: the latent that is supposed to represent an action is instead a body code, because the reconstruction loss monopolises gradient and dynamics loss is too weak to constrain it. This leads to cross‑embodiment transfer failing beyond the convex hull of training bodies, despite the encoder carrying morphology information that generalises. The partial repair you achieved through cross‑decoding—decoding one body’s latent against another\'s frame—illustrates an important principle: changing the question and loss geometry can force the network to use the latent for action and the frame for morphology, breaking the cheap lookup.

Egocentric visual self‑modeling projects this principle into a complete framework. Hu et al. train a self‑supervised, task‑agnostic dynamic self‑model from egocentric video and actions, with loss placed entirely on future state changes, and show that it implicitly learns morphology, kinematics, and dynamics and generalises across robots.[Egocentric visual self-modeling for aut...](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44182-025-00031-6>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03386>)[Egocentric Visual Self-Modeling for Aut...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2207.03386v3>) Chen et al. and Hu et al. further show that robots can learn full‑body simulations of themselves from mirror‑like external cameras and use these simulations for motion planning, damage detection, and recovery.[\[PDF\] Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of...](<https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Full-Body-Visual-Self-Modeling-of-Robot-Chen-Kwiatkowski/42908b31098fd6760fc35e23e77c9c6d3b03e4f9>)[Teaching Robots to Build Simulations of...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12151>)[Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling of Robot...](<https://robot-morphology.cs.columbia.edu/>) These works demonstrate that visual self‑models can replace kinematic priors and URDFs and can serve as world models for control. When combined with model‑based RL techniques that address compounding error and critic stability—AdaMVE, Koopman Dreamer, DreamerV3, MBPO, and robotic world models—you obtain a robust recipe for learning policies from imagined trajectories through self‑models.[\[PDF\] Learning to Combat Compounding-Er...](<https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~chenjun/files/drlw_adambrl_full.pdf>)[Koopman Dreamer: Spectrally Constrained...](<https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19719v2>)[DreamerV3: Mastering Diverse Domains th...](<https://vitalab.github.io/article/2023/01/19/DreamerV3.html>)[When to Trust Your Model: Model-Based P...](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253>)[Physical AIに向けて：Robotic World Model (RWM)](<https://zenn.dev/cybernetics/articles/2bad5339dd9508>)

For your cross‑embodiment locomotion project, the path forward is to adopt egocentric dynamic self‑models per body, define a shared morphology‑agnostic body‑motion coordinate in Froude‑scaled forward/lateral/yaw, redesign the latent to encode action effects under dynamics‑centric loss, treat reconstruction as auxiliary or separate, and embed this into a world‑model‑based RL loop with explicit model trust mechanisms. The architecture and loss choices of Egocentric Visual Self‑Modeling provide the necessary technical detail for the self‑model; world‑model RL literature provides the necessary tools for stable policy learning. Your findings already show that the encoder is capable and that cross‑decoding can reorient the decoder. The remaining challenge is to reshape the latent and loss so that your world model learns “what actions do to bodies” rather than “which body is acting,” across embodiments.

From this perspective, Egocentric Visual Self‑Modeling does not merely “relate” to your work; it offers a technically precise, experimentally validated starting point for solving the issues in FINDINGS.txt and achieving your goal of morphology‑agnostic cross‑embodiment locomotion from vision alone.
